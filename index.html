01 — The experiment

One strain, one stressor, two genomes to compare

Chlorella sp. HS2 is a green, freshwater-tolerant microalga. ALE is its own descendant, produced by prolonged culturing under hydrolysate — the dark, phenolic-and-furan-rich liquid left over after breaking down lignocellulosic biomass, and normally hostile to microbial growth. We assembled both genomes independently from PacBio HiFi long reads and compared them directly, rather than mapping short reads onto a single reference and inferring the rest.

05 — Convergent evolution

Two other lineages, evolved under different stress

HS2 is the shared ancestor of three independently-selected lines, not just ALE. HS19 was selected under sodium hypochlorite (oxidative-chlorine) stress; HMF was selected against 5-hydroxymethylfurfural alone — the specific furanic sub-component of ALE's full hydrolysate cocktail. Comparing all three asks whether unrelated selective pressures converge on the same biology. An initial single-caller comparison found three exact-position matches; none survived re-calling with the same rigor applied everywhere else in this project, and were retracted as convergence evidence rather than kept for a good headline.

09 — A decade of background

Is the ALE signal bigger than normal drift?

A 2015 PacBio assembly of HS2 itself — the same lineage, eleven years earlier, before the ALE experiment existed — gives a rare chance to ask what "normal" looks like: how much does this genome drift under nothing but routine lab culture, and does any of that background noise touch the ALE findings above?

10 — Organelle vs. nuclear mutation rate

Chloroplast, mitochondrion, and nucleus don't change at the same rate

HS2 and ALE were also independently HiFi-assembled at the organelle level — both a chloroplast and a mitochondrial genome per strain — letting us ask the same question three times: how much did each genomic compartment actually change over 18 months of selection?

The pattern, and why it's the opposite of animals

Chloroplast most conserved, mitochondrion intermediate, nucleus fastest-changing — this is the plant/algal pattern, not the animal one (where mitochondrial mutation rate typically exceeds the nuclear rate). Plastid genomes are usually under the strongest purifying selection of any compartment in a photosynthetic eukaryote, which fits the zero-difference chloroplast result cleanly. One honest limit: the organelle side of this rests on very small absolute numbers (7 events across 280 kb total, vs. 51.2 Mb nuclear), so the precise rate ratio carries real statistical uncertainty even though the zero-chloroplast result itself is solid. We also can't yet say whether the 7 mitochondrial changes arose in the ALE lineage specifically or during HS2's own parallel culturing — that needs a temporal organelle series, which doesn't exist yet, unlike the nuclear genome's 3-timepoint resequencing.

ESM2 variant-effect scoring & structure comparison

All four missense candidates scored with ESM2-650M (masked-marginal variant effect prediction, a protein language model score — not a physical simulation) and folded with ESMFold, wild-type vs. mutant, to check whether the structural picture agrees with the sequence-based score. Three of the four (MASY, g5108, g1283) exceed the public ESMFold API's ~400-residue length cap; those were folded as a 380-residue window centered on the mutation site, which is why the long peripheral loops in those renders shouldn't be read as meaningful — confirmed by their own pLDDT confidence (~0.7 at the mutation site vs. ~0.4 at the periphery, PDB B-factor column). g8767 fits under the cap and was folded full-length. Three resolutions are shown for each: whole-fold context, atomic-detail close-up, and a solvent-accessible-surface view with the mutated residue's actual %RSA (relative solvent accessibility, Tien et al. 2013 reference scale) — burial context that a single backbone-overlap image can't give you, and the single most useful number here for guessing at functional consequence.

The one place the evidence disagrees

g1283's F207S sits at 0.5% relative solvent accessibility — essentially zero surface exposure, confirmed visually below (the phenylalanine ring is entirely hidden under the surface). Burying a small polar serine where a bulky hydrophobic phenylalanine ring used to fill the packing pocket is a well-known destabilizing pattern in protein biophysics, even when a single static structure prediction shows a tolerable backbone (which is exactly what ESM2's fold-level score and the close-up overlay both show here). This doesn't overturn the near-neutral call, but it's reason enough to prioritize g1283 alongside g8767 for wet-lab follow-up rather than deprioritizing it on the ESM2 score alone. Windowing makes this finding more credible, not less: truncating the protein can only expose a residue that would otherwise be more buried in the full-length context, never hide one artificially.

12 — Genome engineering targets

Where to build next: candidate landing-pad loci

ALE's advantage — and its lipid-storage bias in particular — is a natural starting point for further metabolic engineering (e.g. pathway genes to push lipid accumulation further). That means identifying landing pads: genomic sites where a construct can be inserted without disrupting anything that matters, and without landing in an unstable or ambiguous region of the assembly.

We screened the purged HS2 assembly against every category of site a landing pad should avoid: all 9,388 AUGUSTUS-predicted genes (ab initio, Chlorella species model — a proper gene prediction, not the redundant Trinity-transcript mapping an earlier pass of this analysis mistakenly relied on), all 30 confirmed HS2/ALE candidate mutations, both confirmed structural variants, 3,014 repeat-artifact-flagged positions, 11,928 self-synteny duplicated blocks, all 76 positions unstable across the 2015-vs-2026 decade comparison, and the first/last 5kb of every contig — each with a buffer zone. Only 14 windows ≥3kb survive — the six largest, on contigs large enough to trust:

Revised after a proper gene prediction — and that revision is the finding

An earlier pass of this analysis, screening only against the 1,495 universal BUSCO essential genes, found windows up to 95 kb and flagged that it couldn't rule out overlap with non-essential genes. Running AUGUSTUS ab initio prediction (9,388 genes genome-wide, a realistic count for this genome size) to close that gap changed the answer substantially: the largest genuinely clear window anywhere in the HS2 genome is 12.7 kb, and among candidates on well-assembled, non-fragmentary contigs, none exceed 7.3 kb. That's not a failure of the search — it's a real property of this genome. A ~51 Mb chlorophyte assembly with 9,388 predicted genes is gene-dense enough that long gene-free intergenic stretches are simply scarce. Practically: a landing pad here should plan around a construct that fits in single-digit kilobases, not tens of kilobases, and ptg000046l:190,455–197,760 (7.3 kb, mid-contig on a 595 kb contig) is the best-supported starting candidate.

13 — Epigenetic signature

A sequence-only trace of where methylation has concentrated

Direct methylation calling needs PacBio kinetics data (IPD/PW values) preserved in the original instrument BAM — this project's raw reads exist only as FASTQ, so that signal is gone and no direct 5mC call is possible here. What sequence composition alone can still show is an indirect, historical proxy: CpG observed/expected ratio (CpG O/E). Methylated cytosines spontaneously deaminate to thymine over evolutionary time, so regions with a long history of methylation accumulate a measurable CpG deficit — a mutational scar, not a live readout.

0.685

What this does and doesn't say

The genome-wide split (genic vs. repeat) is real and consistent with the general pattern documented in green algae: methylation concentrating on repetitive/duplicated DNA more than on genes. It says nothing about whether ALE and HS2 differ from each other — 18 months of divergence is far too short for a new CpG-depletion signature to accumulate, so this proxy is structurally blind to any real epigenetic change that may have happened during the ALE selection experiment itself, in either direction. We also checked whether the landing-pad candidates specifically carry a favorable (gene-like) signature: they don't, one way or the other — a neutral result, reported as neutral rather than left unchecked.

14 — Independent replication

A third-party facility assembled the same reads — and found the same mutations

NICEM (National Instrumentation Center for Environmental Management) independently assembled our raw PacBio HiFi reads from scratch, with no access to our pipeline, and ran their own annotation. One correction for the record: NICEM's own files and reports label our HS2 ancestor strain simply as “HS” — that's a naming shorthand on their end, not a different strain; it refers to the same HS2 line used throughout this project.

Does this mean our purged (“truncated”) genome could be hiding something?

No — if anything, this cross-check argues the opposite. purge_dups removes redundant haplotig copies, not unique sequence: our purged assemblies retain 98.9–99.1% complete single-copy BUSCO genes with only 0.5–1.2% residual duplication, which is exactly what successful purging of a diploid genome should look like, not evidence of lost content. More directly: EPHA, CLPB3, SCP46, and the 16 kb duplication were found independently in NICEM's completely separate, unpurged, differently-assembled contigs — if our purging step had somehow fabricated or masked these loci, they couldn't possibly reappear at the same coordinates in an assembly built by a different team with a different pipeline. Two independent assemblies converging on the same four loci is stronger evidence for them being real than either assembly alone could provide. The one caveat purging can genuinely introduce is at heterozygous sites, where collapsing two haplotigs into one can hide the non-reference allele — a separate issue from truncation, and one already addressed in the temporal cross-check (Section 9).

What's actually inside the 16 kb duplication?

Six genes, identified by local BLASTp against Swiss-Prot with graded confidence. Four are a coherent, high-confidence chromatin block: core histones H3 and H4, a linker histone (H1), and a Myb-family transcription factor. The remaining two are lower-confidence: g1283 (24–27% identity to chitin deacetylases — below the safe-homology threshold; this is the same locus carrying the confirmed ALE missense mutation Phe207Ser, above) and g1287 (32.5% identity to a UBX-domain-containing protein, e-value 4.96×10−70 over a near-full-length 495 aa alignment — a moderate-confidence call, since alignment length and e-value carry more weight than raw %identity for remote homology). Flanking-sequence analysis argues against classical NAHR (non-allelic homologous recombination) as the mechanism: the sequence immediately outside the repeat unit is non-repetitive on both sides, which NAHR — which requires flanking homologous repeats to recombine against — would not produce. A replication-based (FoSTeS/MMBIR) or NHEJ-mediated origin is more consistent with what's observed.

15 — Why it matters

From lab bench to bioprocess: what the advantage is worth

Genotype explains the mechanism; phenotype proves it works. The last question is whether it's worth anything outside a flask. A screening-level techno-economic and carbon-footprint analysis, built from the same measured biomass yields above and literature-anchored unit costs, says yes — concentrated exactly where it should be.

Scope, stated plainly

Both models above cover cultivation only — feedstock and illumination, no harvesting, downstream processing, or capital cost. They are screening-level estimates built to show where the strain-productivity advantage does and doesn't translate into economic or environmental value, not a bankable process design.

Extending to the full bioprocess: algal oil

The same measured DCW and total-lipid yield, chained through two additional literature-anchored stages — dewatering (centrifugation) and solvent extraction — through to crude algal (Chlorella) oil, the actual sellable intermediate. This is the most defensible extension beyond cultivation alone: it adds only two external, literature-only stages to data we measured ourselves.

Supplementary: biodiesel vs. SAF — an illustrative, lower-confidence extension

Going one stage further, to a finished fuel, requires distinguishing three genuinely different products rather than treating "biofuel" as one thing: crude algal oil needs no upgrading; biodiesel (FAME) requires transesterification (methanol + catalyst) and competes with fossil road diesel (RED II baseline, 94 gCO2e/MJ); SAF requires a fundamentally different hydroprocessing chemistry — either a dedicated HEFA unit (H2-driven deoxygenation) or refinery co-processing — and competes with fossil jet fuel (ICAO CORSIA baseline, 89 gCO2e/MJ). Fuel-quality metrics reported earlier (cetane number, iodine value) describe diesel-relevant properties, not jet-fuel specs — worth keeping distinct.
