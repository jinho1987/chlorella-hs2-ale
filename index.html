01 — The experiment

One strain, one stressor, two genomes to compare

Chlorella sp. HS2 is a green, freshwater-tolerant microalga. ALE is its own descendant, produced by prolonged culturing under hydrolysate — the dark, phenolic-and-furan-rich liquid left over after breaking down lignocellulosic biomass, and normally hostile to microbial growth. We assembled both genomes independently from PacBio HiFi long reads and compared them directly, rather than mapping short reads onto a single reference and inferring the rest.

02 — Phenotype

Eighteen months of selection produced a measurably different alga

ALE was evolved from HS2 under light, 1:1-diluted 0.5M HCl hydrolysate (defatted Chlorella biomass acid-hydrolyzed at 0.5M HCl, then neutralized with NaOH before use) — this is the selection condition every comparison below is read against. Ratios throughout are hydrolysate : water, so 3:1 is the most concentrated (harshest) and 1:3 the most dilute (mildest); 1:1 is the exact condition ALE was evolved under.

Everything below follows the same two-axis design: (1) a light dose-response across the full 3:1 / 1:1 / 1:3 range plus a YM (standard yeast-malt, 10 g/L dextrose) no-stress control, to test whether the phenotype generalizes beyond the exact selection dose; and (2) a light-vs-dark comparison at the matched 1:1 dose only, to test whether the phenotype needs light. Dark cultivation was deliberately run at just this one matched dose, not the full range — light and dark are not symmetric datasets, and every figure below states which axis it belongs to. An earlier 0.25M HCl acid-strength condition was tested early on but is not used anywhere on this page going forward — 0.5M HCl is the sole hydrolysate condition of record. Two further additions are planned: a YM-based NaCl and 5-HMF isolated-stressor tolerance test (this month), directly following up on the genomic sections' finding that ALE's mutations don't map to 5-HMF exposure alone; and single-cell Raman spectroscopy, targeted specifically at lipid saturation/unsaturation profiling (C=C stretch and CH2/CH3 band ratios) to corroborate the FAME-derived MUFA/PUFA shift already found below at single-cell resolution.

05 — Convergent evolution

Two other lineages, evolved under different stress

HS2 is the shared ancestor of three independently-selected lines, not just ALE. HS19 was selected under sodium hypochlorite (oxidative-chlorine) stress; HMF was selected against 5-hydroxymethylfurfural alone — the specific furanic sub-component of ALE's full hydrolysate cocktail. Comparing all three asks whether unrelated selective pressures converge on the same biology. An initial single-caller comparison found three exact-position matches; none survived re-calling with the same rigor applied everywhere else in this project, and were retracted as convergence evidence rather than kept for a good headline.

Scope note: the manuscript's core comparison is ALE vs. HS2. HS19 and HMF are supporting evidence for one specific question — whether ALE's candidate mutations are a 5-HMF-specific response or reflect the broader hydrolysate matrix — not a second axis of the paper. Treat this section as a specificity check for that argument, not as a parallel three-way comparison carrying equal narrative weight.

One funnel criterion HMF genuinely can't have

ALE's funnel includes a reciprocal-direction check (re-confirming each candidate using ALE's own genome as the mapping reference), which requires a long-read assembly of the mutant strain. HMF has none — it exists only as short-read data. Replicate concordance (both independent resequencing replicates must agree, on both callers) substitutes for that missing criterion; it tests the same underlying principle (a real signal should be reproducible from independent evidence) but is not identical to it, and is disclosed as a difference rather than presented as equivalent rigor.

A second funnel-consistency bug, caught before publishing on it

This funnel's "not standing HS2 heterozygosity" step originally checked only one of three independent HS2 self-resequencing batches (b2). Checking all three (the same standard applied to the ALE audit above) found 12 of the original 19 candidates present in at least one of the other two batches — including a position in g1361 (Ribosomal oxygenase 1) that briefly looked like real HMF–HS19 convergence until this check caught it as the same kind of baseline artifact. The funnel-step numbers above (14 → 7) are the corrected values; the original single-baseline numbers were 145 → 19. Every HMF result on this page reflects the corrected 7-candidate set.

A real artifact caught mid-analysis, not swept past

The first pass of this funnel produced 71 "sure candidates" — but 52 of them sat in dense clusters (up to 30 calls within 28 kb) entirely on short, fragmentary contigs (10–40 kb, versus 267 kb+ for every other candidate's contig). That's the signature of a repeat-attractor artifact, not real biology, so a contig-length filter (≥100 kb) was added and disclosed here rather than left in the final count.

Convergence check: zero overlap, at either resolution

None of these 7 HMF sure candidates share an exact position with any of ALE's sure candidates (24, after the funnel-gap audit in §11). None fall inside the same AUGUSTUS gene body as any of ALE's 9 named candidates or 3 named missense sites either — checked directly against the gene models, not just by symbol. Combined with the MOCOS/GLOX retraction above, this reprocessed, more complete HMF dataset shows no reproducible convergence with ALE at all, at either the position or gene level.

Read plainly against the project's original 5-HMF-driver hypothesis: if ALE's candidate mutations were primarily a response to the 5-HMF component of hydrolysate stress specifically, adapting to 5-HMF alone should hit at least some of the same genes. It doesn't — which argues that ALE's specific candidate set is more likely shaped by other components of the hydrolysate mixture (sugars, other inhibitory compounds, osmotic load) than by 5-HMF in isolation. That's a real, useful negative result, not an absence of one.

Coding consequence: no folding targets in this set

Each of the 7 HMF sure candidates was run through the same CDS-splicing / codon-translation classifier used for ALE's 24 (gene model → strand-aware CDS assembly → codon lookup → translation, with indels separately checked for frame effect). Result: 4 intronic, 3 intergenic — zero missense, zero synonymous, zero nonsense. None of HMF's candidates alter a coding sequence, so unlike ALE's three confirmed missense hits, none warrant ESM2/ESMFold structural comparison. Combined with the zero position/gene overlap above, HMF's adaptation to 5-HMF alone appears to run almost entirely through non-coding sites — a different molecular route than ALE's, not just a different gene set.

So what does a null result against HMF actually mean for ALE?

Not that ALE's genome is unexplained noise. ALE's ale_only candidates — the 11 confirmed absent from HS2's own resequencing across the board, including g8767 (L82P) — are real, ALE-specific changes regardless of what HMF does or doesn't share. What the HMF result adds is specificity: adapting to 5-HMF alone reproduces none of ALE's candidate set, at the position, gene, pathway, or mutation-mechanism level. The most defensible interpretation is that ALE adapted to the full hydrolysate cocktail — sugars, other furanics and phenolics, osmotic/ionic load — and 5-HMF in isolation is not sufficient to explain ALE's genomic signature, whether because it's a minor contributor or because it only matters in combination with the rest of the mixture. That keeps the ALE-vs-HS2 comparison standing on its own as a self-contained case study; it also means one of its more interesting internal claims (5-HMF being read as a plausible single driver) was tested directly rather than left as an assumption, and didn't hold up. (An earlier version of this section leaned on the CLCN7/HS19 convergence hit as additional evidence against drift — that hit has since been retracted, below, as a reference-assembly artifact, so this argument now rests on the HMF-only comparison and the confirmed ale_only candidates alone, not on cross-stressor convergence.)

A reference mismatch caught while building this comparison — and a second, bigger problem found along the way

Building the coordinate-matched comparison above required checking whether HS19's existing variant calls sit on the same footing as ALE's and HMF's. They didn't: HS19 was originally called against HS2_polished.fa (327 contigs, pre–purge_dups) with Trinity-transcript-based gene naming, while ALE and HMF are both called against HS2_purged_v2.purged.fa (78 contigs, ~249 duplicate/haplotig contigs removed) with AUGUSTUS gene IDs. HS19 has since been realigned and re-called against the corrected, shared reference, using the same funnel logic applied to HMF (results below). That re-verification is also what surfaced the CLCN7 problem: checking it properly required auditing ALE's own funnel, which turned up a real gap affecting 19 of ALE's 30 original sure candidates — see the audit callout in §11 for the full account, including which candidates are retracted and which are reframed.

HS19, reprocessed: convergence with ALE, checked properly this time

HS19 was realigned and re-called from scratch against the corrected HS2_purged_v2.purged.fa reference (97.7% mapped), then run through the same GATK + bcftools funnel used for HMF. HS19 has no biological replicate, which is a bigger limitation than HMF's two-replicate design — caller-concordance alone let through a large number of short-read indel-calling artifacts in long homopolymer/microsatellite tracts (the same failure mode flagged for HMF's indels, but far more of them here). An additional homopolymer-adjacency filter (excluding indels next to a run ≥6bp) was added and disclosed as an explicit extra criterion, the same way the contig-length filter was added for HMF.

Zero convergence with ALE, same as HMF — but HS19 does have its own missense mutations

Unlike HMF (zero missense among 7 candidates), HS19's 95 sure candidates classify as 80 intronic, 4 synonymous, 5 missense, 5 intergenic, and 1 frameshift indel. None of the missense genes (GAPDH-like g1079, La-related protein g3355, a glucuronoxylan glucuronosyltransferase g5894, a receptor-like kinase g8574) overlap ALE's gene set or functional themes. So HS19 clearly does undergo coding-sequence adaptation under its own stressor — it just doesn't overlap ALE's, reinforcing that the zero-convergence result for both HMF and HS19 reflects real evolutionary specificity, not a general inability of any of these three lines to acquire missense mutations.

A mutation-spectrum signature that matches the stressor's chemistry

HS19's mutation spectrum is dominated by C>A (51.4% of 74 SNVs) — the classic signature of 8-oxoguanine oxidative DNA damage, and if anything stronger after the baseline correction than before. HS19 was evolved under NaOCl (sodium hypochlorite) selection, a strong oxidizer, so this is a mechanistically coherent match between stressor chemistry and mutational signature — distinct from both ALE (T>G/T>C-dominant, 43.5%/26.1%) and HMF (too few SNVs left after correction, n=4, to characterize a spectrum). Two of the three lines show a clear, distinct signature; the third (HMF) simply doesn't have enough data yet to say.

Structural variants: do either of ALE's two confirmed SVs recur in HMF or HS19?

ALE carries two confirmed structural variants (§14): a 16 kb tandem duplication and a ~1.5 kb insertion. Checked in HMF (both replicates) and HS19 two ways: read-depth at the breakpoint vs. single-copy flanking controls (a real duplication should show roughly double the flanking depth; a real deletion, roughly half), and independently with Delly v0.8.3, a dedicated short-read SV caller, run on HMF1.

No evidence of either SV in HMF or HS19, by either method

Duplication locus (ptg000004l:319003–335190): depth is flat and matches the flanking single-copy controls in HMF1, HMF2, and HS19 alike (e.g. HS19: 140.5× in the region vs. 133.5–140.0× in the flanks) — no doubling, in any of the three. Insertion locus (ptg000012l:2064929): depth is likewise close to flanking controls in all three samples, with no consistent, direction-matching signal. Delly, run independently on both HMF replicates, agrees: HMF1 produced 24,897 raw SV calls, HMF2 38,965 (both dominated by low-support inversions — typical unfiltered short-read SV noise, not usable as a clean list without substantial further filtering), and neither calls anything precise at either exact ALE breakpoint. Three independent methods, two replicates, two loci, all converging on the same answer: these two SVs are ALE-specific, not shared with either independently-evolved line, consistent with the zero convergence already found at the SNV/indel level.

The pattern, and why it's the opposite of animals

Chloroplast most conserved, mitochondrion intermediate, nucleus fastest-changing — this is the plant/algal pattern, not the animal one (where mitochondrial mutation rate typically exceeds the nuclear rate). Plastid genomes are usually under the strongest purifying selection of any compartment in a photosynthetic eukaryote, which fits the zero-difference chloroplast result cleanly. One honest limit: the organelle side of this rests on very small absolute numbers (7 events across 280 kb total, vs. 51.2 Mb nuclear), so the precise rate ratio carries real statistical uncertainty even though the zero-chloroplast result itself is solid. We also can't yet say whether the 7 mitochondrial changes arose in the ALE lineage specifically or during HS2's own parallel culturing — that needs a temporal organelle series, which doesn't exist yet, unlike the nuclear genome's 3-timepoint resequencing.

Mutational signature ("mutation grammar"): sharpening the nuclear result, and an honest organelle comparison

The genome-wide C>A/G>T enrichment reported earlier resolves, at trinucleotide resolution, into a specific, named signature rather than a generic transversion excess.

Flanking-sequence spec updated: ±600bp, not ±300bp

The primer cards below were designed using ±300bp of flanking template on each side of the variant. The primer sequences and product sizes shown below still reflect the older ±300bp design and are pending redesign to this standard. A funnel gap found on audit: 19 of 30 "sure candidates" never actually had their HS2-baseline cleanliness checked

Re-verifying convergence claims for the HS19 comparison (below) required auditing ALE's own funnel logic directly. Finding: the funnel's short-read-support check only verifies "absent from HS2's own resequencing" for candidates in the ale_only category (11 of 30). For the shifted category (19 of 30, including MASY, g1283, g5108, and CLCN7) that check was silently skipped — a real gap in the original pipeline, not a data problem. Auditing all 19 directly against three independent HS2 self-resequencing batches (b1/b2/b3) gives three outcomes:

Retracted (6 of 19), including MASY and CLCN7: HS2 itself is homozygous for the "mutant" allele in all 3 independent batches, identical to ALE's genotype. Ancestor and descendant match exactly — this is not an ALE-specific change at all, most likely a reference-assembly base-call error. MASY is removed from the missense set below; CLCN7 is retracted as a convergence candidate (see §05).

Reframed, not retracted (7 of 19), including g1283 and g5108: HS2 is consistently heterozygous (~45–80% alt allele fraction, all 3 batches) at these positions, and ALE is homozygous or near-fixed. That's a real, measurable allele-frequency shift — but it means these are cases of a pre-existing heterozygous HS2 variant becoming fixed in ALE, not a new mutation that arose during evolution. The amino-acid change and structural analysis below remain valid; the evolutionary story changes from "ALE evolved this" to "ALE fixed a variant HS2 already carried."

Ambiguous (3 of 19): low-depth or complex multiallelic calls on both sides, not confidently classifiable either way with current data.

Net effect: the funnel-validated set drops from 30 to 24 sure candidates (11 ale_only, unaffected, plus 13 of the 19 shifted candidates surviving with the 6 false positives removed), with 7 of those 24 recharacterized as fixation-of-standing-variation rather than novel mutation. This is disclosed here in full rather than silently corrected, consistent with how the MOCOS/GLOX retraction above was handled.

12 — Genome engineering targets

Where to build next: candidate landing-pad loci

ALE's advantage — and its lipid-storage bias in particular — is a natural starting point for further metabolic engineering (e.g. pathway genes to push lipid accumulation further). That means identifying landing pads: genomic sites where a construct can be inserted without disrupting anything that matters, and without landing in an unstable or ambiguous region of the assembly.

We screened the purged HS2 assembly against every category of site a landing pad should avoid: all 9,388 AUGUSTUS-predicted genes (ab initio, Chlorella species model — a proper gene prediction, not the redundant Trinity-transcript mapping an earlier pass of this analysis mistakenly relied on), all 30 confirmed HS2/ALE candidate mutations, both confirmed structural variants, 3,014 repeat-artifact-flagged positions, 11,928 self-synteny duplicated blocks, all 76 positions unstable across the 2015-vs-2026 decade comparison, and the first/last 5kb of every contig — each with a buffer zone. Revised after a proper gene prediction — and that revision is the finding

An earlier pass of this analysis, screening only against the 1,495 universal BUSCO essential genes, found windows up to 95 kb and flagged that it couldn't rule out overlap with non-essential genes. Practically: a landing pad here should plan around a construct that fits in single-digit kilobases, not tens of kilobases, and ptg000046l:190,455–197,760 (7.3 kb, mid-contig on a 595 kb contig) is the best-supported starting candidate.

13 — Epigenetic signature

A sequence-only trace of where methylation has concentrated

Direct methylation calling needs PacBio kinetics data (IPD/PW values) preserved in the original instrument BAM — this project's raw reads exist only as FASTQ, so that signal is gone and no direct 5mC call is possible here. What sequence composition alone can still show is an indirect, historical proxy: CpG observed/expected ratio (CpG O/E). Methylated cytosines spontaneously deaminate to thymine over evolutionary time, so regions with a long history of methylation accumulate a measurable CpG deficit — a mutational scar, not a live readout.

What this does and doesn't say

It says nothing about whether ALE and HS2 differ from each other — 18 months of divergence is far too short for a new CpG-depletion signature to accumulate, so this proxy is structurally blind to any real epigenetic change that may have happened during the ALE selection experiment itself, in either direction. We also checked whether the landing-pad candidates specifically carry a favorable (gene-like) signature: they don't, one way or the other — a neutral result, reported as neutral rather than left unchecked.

Does this mean our purged (“truncated”) genome could be hiding something?

No — if anything, this cross-check argues the opposite. purge_dups removes redundant haplotig copies, not unique sequence: our purged assemblies retain 98.9–99.1% complete single-copy BUSCO genes with only 0.5–1.2% residual duplication, which is exactly what successful purging of a diploid genome should look like, not evidence of lost content. More directly: EPHA, CLPB3, SCP46, and the 16 kb duplication were found independently in NICEM's completely separate, unpurged, differently-assembled contigs — if our purging step had somehow fabricated or masked these loci, they couldn't possibly reappear at the same coordinates in an assembly built by a different team with a different pipeline. Two independent assemblies converging on the same four loci is stronger evidence for them being real than either assembly alone could provide. The one caveat purging can genuinely introduce is at heterozygous sites, where collapsing two haplotigs into one can hide the non-reference allele — a separate issue from truncation, and one already addressed in the temporal cross-check (Section 9).

What's actually inside the 16 kb duplication?

Six genes, identified by local BLASTp against Swiss-Prot with graded confidence. Four are a coherent, high-confidence chromatin block: core histones H3 and H4, a linker histone (H1), and a Myb-family transcription factor. The remaining two are lower-confidence: g1283 (24–27% identity to chitin deacetylases — below the safe-homology threshold; this is the same locus carrying the Phe207Ser variant discussed above — now understood to be a pre-existing HS2 heterozygous variant fixed in ALE, not a novel mutation, see the audit callout in §11) and g1287 (32.5% identity to a UBX-domain-containing protein, e-value 4.96×10−70 over a near-full-length 495 aa alignment — a moderate-confidence call, since alignment length and e-value carry more weight than raw %identity for remote homology). Flanking-sequence analysis argues against classical NAHR (non-allelic homologous recombination) as the mechanism: the sequence immediately outside the repeat unit is non-repetitive on both sides, which NAHR — which requires flanking homologous repeats to recombine against — would not produce. A replication-based (FoSTeS/MMBIR) or NHEJ-mediated origin is more consistent with what's observed.

A structural-variant folding target: the duplication doesn't just double g1283, it also truncates a second copy of it

The "six genes" above are the ones cleanly contained inside the duplicated block. Checking the exact breakpoints against the gene models turns up something not previously examined: the start breakpoint (319,003) falls inside g1283 rather than between genes, and the end breakpoint (335,190) falls inside a seventh gene, g1288 (not part of the six-gene count above, since only its outermost 169 bp is touched). Checking the internal copy-to-copy junction sequence directly — the base pairs where the end of the first duplicated copy joins the start of the second — shows an in-frame stop codon sitting exactly at that boundary (both breakpoints happen to fall precisely at a codon boundary, phase 0, in both genes). So no g1283–g1288 fusion protein forms; that possibility is ruled out directly rather than assumed.

What the duplication does produce: ALE carries two different copies of g1283 side by side — one complete, normal 536-residue copy (reconstituted from the single-copy flank plus the first repeat unit, carrying the already-discussed F207S variant), and one truncated second copy, cut off after residue 341 (63.6% of the full length), missing the entire C-terminal third (195 residues) to the premature stop at the internal junction. This truncated protein doesn't exist in HS2 at all — it's a novel structural product created directly by the SV itself, not by any point mutation, and a legitimate folding target in its own right.

15 — Why it matters

From lab bench to bioprocess: what the advantage is worth

Genotype explains the mechanism; phenotype proves it works. The last question is whether it's worth anything outside a flask. A screening-level techno-economic and carbon-footprint analysis, built from the same measured biomass yields above and literature-anchored unit costs, says yes — concentrated exactly where it should be.

The sharpest claim this data actually supports: a closed-loop, recirculating substrate

The hydrolysate in every experiment on this page comes from acid-treating defatted Chlorella biomass — the leftover solids after lipid extraction, not a separately-sourced feedstock. That makes the growth-advantage result a concrete process-design argument, not just a stress-tolerance curiosity: ALE grows substantially better than HS2 specifically on the byproduct of its own biomass's lipid extraction, at every hydrolysate concentration tested, in both light and dark. That's the empirical basis for a closed-loop, recirculating mixotrophic/heterotrophic cultivation scheme — defatted residual biomass hydrolyzed back into growth substrate for the next cultivation cycle, rather than discarded as waste. This is deliberately the conservative claim: it doesn't require the furanic/salt mechanism to be resolved, doesn't depend on which specific mutation is causal, and is already directly supported by measured dry-cell-weight yields and the cost/carbon figures above — the kind of claim that survives even if every open genomic question here stays open.

Scope, stated plainly

Both models above cover cultivation only — feedstock and illumination, no harvesting, downstream processing, or capital cost. They are screening-level estimates built to show where the strain-productivity advantage does and doesn't translate into economic or environmental value, not a bankable process design.

Extending to the full bioprocess: algal oil

The same measured DCW and total-lipid yield, chained through two additional literature-anchored stages — dewatering (centrifugation) and solvent extraction — through to crude algal (Chlorella) oil, the actual sellable intermediate. This is the most defensible extension beyond cultivation alone: it adds only two external, literature-only stages to data we measured ourselves.

Supplementary: biodiesel vs. SAF — an illustrative, lower-confidence extension

Going one stage further, to a finished fuel, requires distinguishing three genuinely different products rather than treating "biofuel" as one thing: crude algal oil needs no upgrading; biodiesel (FAME) requires transesterification (methanol + catalyst) and competes with fossil road diesel (RED II baseline, 94 gCO2e/MJ); SAF requires a fundamentally different hydroprocessing chemistry — either a dedicated HEFA unit (H2-driven deoxygenation) or refinery co-processing — and competes with fossil jet fuel (ICAO CORSIA baseline, 89 gCO2e/MJ). Fuel-quality metrics reported earlier (cetane number, iodine value) describe diesel-relevant properties, not jet-fuel specs — worth keeping distinct.
